# PASSES — Parameterized AeroStructural State-Estimation Sandbox

A fixed-grid spectral formulation for coupled aeroelastic, thermal, and stochastic GNC flight simulation.

**This repository contains two research manuscripts, their bibliographies, and a complete implementation of the roadmap — every kernel of both papers, assembled into a coupled single-trajectory simulator. Twenty of the twenty-two verification tasks are executed and passing; the remaining two (I-V4, II-V8) pass every leg executable from this repository, with only their published-reference-data comparisons outstanding.** The papers present a mathematical formulation and a falsifiable verification plan written *before* any implementation existed. **Both manuscripts have since been updated in place**: measured values are quoted inline where a claim has been verified, and where implementation showed a claim to be wrong it is corrected with the correction identified as such — most substantially the Tauber–Sutton exponent in Paper II §4.2 and the rigid/elastic split of the structural coordinate in Paper I §3.5. Measured results for every executed task live in [`results/`](results/). Please read the [status table](#status) before drawing conclusions about what works.

## The idea in one section

Coupled multi-physics flight simulation conventionally partitions the problem: a structural solver, a thermal solver, and a trajectory propagator, each on its own mesh, exchanging boundary data. This costs you twice.

**Interpolation error at every interface.** Surface pressure, heat flux, and displacement must be interpolated between meshes. The interpolated field is generally no smoother than $C^0$ across element boundaries, and the aerothermal closures take several derivatives of it.

**Remeshing.** Ablative recession and large deformation are handled by moving-boundary formulations with periodic mesh regeneration. That is expensive, hard to vectorize, and introduces a projection step between pre- and post-remesh states. Tolerable for one deterministic trajectory; prohibitive for the $10^3$–$10^4$ replicates needed to resolve a dispersion footprint.

PASSES discretizes every spatial domain **once**, on a fixed grid, and holds it fixed for the entire trajectory. Three formulations make that possible:

- **Structural.** Free-free boundary conditions are imposed by projecting onto the null space of the constraint operator rather than by replacing rows. Row replacement destroys operator symmetry and produces spurious growing modes; projection does not.
- **Thermal.** A Landau coordinate transformation maps the receding physical domain onto a fixed computational domain. The ablation front is not eliminated from the physics — it is rendered *stationary in computational coordinates*, so no node is ever created, destroyed, or interpolated. The cost is one explicit advection term.
- **Coupling.** Localized slosh forces are regularized by a Gaussian kernel normalized against the collocation quadrature rule, so total force transfers exactly rather than to within quadrature error.

The result is one system of ODEs with fixed dimension and fixed sparsity. **That is the whole computational argument:** a Monte Carlo batch becomes a rank-3 tensor operation (replicate × state × stage) with no per-replicate remesh, so the batch never decoheres. A moving-mesh formulation cannot offer this, because each replicate's mesh diverges from every other's after its first remesh event.

They were written before any results existed, so the plan is falsifiable in advance rather than reportable selectively afterward. Measured findings worth flagging against the papers' expectations:

- **Conditioning (V1).** The raw $\kappa_2(\hat{\mathbf{K}})$ is pinned at $\sim 1/\varepsilon$ at every $N$, because the free-free operator retains its two *physical* rigid-body null directions. The informative measurand is the elastic condition number $\sigma_1/\sigma_{n-2}$, whose fitted slope is $\approx N^{8.0}$ for both uniform and stepped profiles — the projection removes the constraint-violating extremal modes (and passes the $10^{-6}$ frequency criterion at $N=32$ with $10^{-9}$ to spare) but does not flatten the asymptotic growth rate, which Remark 3 of Paper I deliberately declined to predict.
- **Rigid-mode floor.** The two rigid eigenvalues compute to $\sim 10^{-15}$ relative to $\lambda_{\max}$, occasionally negative. Any long-horizon integration of the *unmodified* reduced operator therefore drifts at rate $\sqrt{|\lambda_{\text{rigid}}|}$ regardless of integrator; modal truncation or rigid-mode deflation handles it, and the V3 comparison accounts for it explicitly.
- **Slosh moment error (V2).** The interior first-moment error for a *resolved* kernel sits near the rounding floor — far below the $\mathcal{O}(\sigma^2)$ allowance of the remark after Prop. 1 — because Clenshaw–Curtis integrates a resolved Gaussian's first moment spectrally. The $\mathcal{O}(\sigma^2)$-scale bias appears exactly where the paper localizes it: stations within $\sim 2\sigma$ of an endpoint (measured $2.2\times 10^{-3}$ relative at $x_s/\sigma = 2$, growing monotonically as the station approaches the end). Force transfer is exact everywhere, worst case $1.5\times 10^{-16}$ relative.
- **Density-rate convention (V4).** Eqs. (3.17)–(3.18) as printed source the gas continuity and pyrolysis enthalpy with $\partial\rho/\partial t|_\eta$ — the full computational-frame rate including grid advection — where the CMA lineage uses the material-frame Arrhenius rate alone; the two differ at $\mathcal{O}(\dot s\,\rho_\eta/\ell)$. The solver implements the paper's letter by default and exposes the choice as an explicit option (`density_rate_convention`) rather than deciding silently; the MMS verifies the default.
- **Filter calibration and recovery (V5).** The χ² gate fires at $9.8\times 10^{-4}$ against the design $p = 10^{-3}$ over $5\times 10^5$ nominal gate evaluations, and no replicate diverges in any of 27 $(N_w, \alpha_{\max}, p)$ configurations — the structural claim of Remark 8 that scalar inflation cannot destabilize the filter. IAE recovers from a 30 m/s separation transient in a median 1.30 s versus 2.15 s for the fixed-Q filter on identical measurements.
- **Batch scaling (V8).** GPU throughput ramps with log–log slope 1.00 in $N_{\mathrm{MC}}$ below saturation and peaks at ~85k replicates/s on the entry workload — 5× the vectorized CPU batch and ~380× a per-replicate Python loop (the decohered execution model a moving-mesh formulation forces). The batched IMEX Newmark structural block sustains millions of replicate-steps/s through one shared LU factorization, which is the §5.2 batching argument made measurable.
- **The conditioning claim, quantified (II-V1).** On the *same* fourth-order variable-$EI$ operator, the ultraspherical banded interior conditions as $\mathcal{O}(N^{1.01})$ where dense collocation gives $\mathcal{O}(N^{8})$ — a ratio of $7.8\times 10^{7}$ at $N = 64$. Under the leading-diagonal right preconditioner the interior $\kappa \le 13$ at every $N$ tested, reproducing the Olver–Townsend $\mathcal{O}(1)$ statement. The assembled *bivariate* Mindlin–Reissner block operator is likewise essentially $\mathcal{O}(1)$: $\kappa$ moves only from 101 to 108 while the problem grows five-fold. The caveat Paper II's Remark raises is confirmed on both: the dense boundary rows — not the interior, and not the block coupling — are what costs conditioning.
- **Conditioning is a statement about scaling, not just discretization (II-V1).** Measured with column equilibration alone the block operator appears to grow as $N^{1.6}$, which would fail the stated criterion. It does not: a block operator mixes entries in different physical units — transverse shear stiffness in N/m against bending rigidity in N·m, several decades apart — and one-sided scaling leaves that imbalance in the matrix. Standard two-sided (Ruiz) equilibration removes it and recovers $N^{0.09}$. The verification report prints both, so the preprocessing choice is visible rather than buried.
- **Free-free is not a BVP (II-V1).** Free-free boundary conditions leave the rigid-body null space, so the bordered free-free system is *singular by construction* — the configuration is a generalized eigenproblem, not a boundary-value problem. The solver detects this and says so rather than returning a factorization of a singular matrix; the bordered-conditioning sweep therefore uses clamped conditions.
- **An exact reference beats a tabulated one (II-V3).** Paper II specifies *published Rayleigh–Ritz values* for the free-free plate as V3's reference, but this repository cannot audit those numbers. Deriving the closed-form Mindlin solution for the **simply-supported** plate instead — separable in $(p,q)$, reducing to a symmetric $3\times 3$ pencil per mode — gives a reference that is exact, self-contained, and exercises the identical operator, boundary machinery, projection and eigensolve. Against it the error contracts exponentially to $3.7\times 10^{-10}$ at $N = 20$, clearing the $10^{-5}$ criterion by five orders of magnitude.
- **Free and supported perimeters fail differently at the corners (II-V1/V3).** The free-edge condition set is rank deficient by exactly **four** — the twist condition $M_{xy} = 0$ belongs to the free-edge triple of *both* edges meeting at each corner, while $M_x \ne M_y$ and $Q_x \ne Q_y$ are independent. A simply-supported perimeter is deficient by **twelve**. Neither number was assumed: the code measures the rank and fails loudly if it lands outside what four corners can produce, so a wrong boundary assembly surfaces immediately instead of as quietly wrong frequencies.
- **Free-free plates converge algebraically, and that is physical (II-V3).** The simply-supported spectrum converges exponentially; the free-free spectrum does not, because free-edge plates carry weak corner singularities. This is a property of the problem, not the discretization — which is exactly why the verdict rests on the exact reference and the free-free comparison against circulated values carries no verdict.
- **No shear locking across three decades (II-V2).** The fundamental frequency parameter falls from 11.71 at $h/L = 0.2$ (genuine thick-section shear softening) to a plateau of 13.307, and at $h/L = 10^{-3}$ differs from that plateau by $+0.018\%$ — against a 1% criterion. Paper II's Remark claims high-order spectral discretizations are "markedly less susceptible" to locking but explicitly declines to claim immunity; this measures it. The cost that *is* paid as sections thin is conditioning: the shear-to-bending stiffness ratio grows as $h^{-2}$, and the rigid-body modes separate from the elastic spectrum by correspondingly fewer decades.
- **The blend is load-neutral (II-V4).** Trim incidence moves by $0.027°$ across the full blend-width sweep — from the unblended $C^0$ closure out to a $2.3°$ half-width — against a $0.1°$ criterion, and the offset grows monotonically and vanishes as $\delta_{\mathrm{blend}} \to 0$. The seam the blend exists to fix is confirmed first: the unblended closure is continuous to $10^{-16}$ but its slope jumps, the Newtonian branch having zero slope at $\delta_c = 0$ where the expansion branch does not.
- **Freezing the structural block wins, and by how much (II-V5).** Carrying a 100 rad/s structural mode through a 300 s coast costs **15× more right-hand-side evaluations** than freezing it once its modal energy has decayed to $10^{-6}$ of initial — with both strategies holding energy to better than $10^{-8}$. Paper II's Remark 5 declined to call the trade in advance; the measurement calls it for freezing, and notes the saving *grows* with coast duration because the ring-down time is fixed while the coast is not. The switch is defensible only because the freeze condition is checked rather than assumed.
- **A quadratic blackout model under-predicts by 13× (II-V6).** All three covariance channels reproduce their $t^3$, $t^4$ and $t^6$ exponents to better than $10^{-6}$, measured from a Lyapunov propagation that carries the biases and tilt as *states* and so shares no code with the closed form. The operational consequence the paper's Remark asserts is now quantified: at two minutes of blackout the gyro channel carries 88% of the variance and a $t^2$ model under-predicts position uncertainty **13-fold**. A pull-up trigger sized on the quadratic model fires far too late.
- **Chattering is structural, not a tuning problem (II-V6).** A bare threshold on $\omega_p \ge \omega_{\mathrm{GNSS}}$ produces 966 transitions on a signal that merely hovers at the boundary. No choice of threshold fixes that — only hysteresis does, and a 10% reacquisition margin reduces it to the single legitimate latch. The gate is therefore hysteretic by construction.
- **The $\ell_1$ penalty is exact; $\ell_2$ is not (II-V7).** On the *same* linearized subproblem, the $\ell_1$ penalty drives $\|\bm{\nu}\|_1$ to **exactly 0.0** at every weight from $w_\nu = 3$ upward — a finite threshold, visible between 1 and 3 — while a quadratic penalty gives 92 → 12 → 1.3 over three decades of weight and then *grows again* as the solver loses the ill-conditioned problem. Both halves are the paper's argument: $\ell_2$ approaches zero only asymptotically, and the weight that makes it small is the weight that wrecks conditioning.
- **A modeling bug the coupled run caught (item 13).** The free-free spectrum's three zero-frequency modes are rigid translation and rotation, which the 6-DOF block already carries. Integrating them in the *modal* block too double-counts rigid motion, and because they have no restoring term the modal coordinate grows without bound under any steady load — it reached $10^7$ before the integrator failed. The structural state of Eq. (3.20) is the *elastic* deformation; only the coupled assembly exposes this, because each kernel is correct on its own.
- **Implicit beats adaptive, decisively (item 13).** With per-component tolerances the coupled system costs ~1970 right-hand-side evaluations under BDF, **flat across a 19× range of $\omega_{\max}$** (265 → 4973 rad/s) — the Prop. 2 constraint removed on the full assembly, not just the structural block. LSODA, nominally a stiffness-switching method, does not switch here and needs $10^5$–$10^6$ evaluations for the same arc. A single scalar `atol` cannot serve a state spanning nine orders of magnitude in units, and using one costs three orders of magnitude in work.
- **Blunting is a genuine trade, on published data (II-V8).** With convective heating falling as $R_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1/2}$ and the published Tauber–Sutton correlation rising as $R_{\mathrm{eff}}^{+a}$, the total heating has an *interior* optimum **inside the source's own 0.3–3 m nose-radius envelope**, at a condition where radiation exceeds convection — the regime the correlation was written for. A convection-only framework drives the radius to the top of the range, which is the over-blunting bias Paper II predicts. The Lewis-exponent choice moves the Fay–Riddell bracket by 1.07% between equilibrium and frozen/catalytic, confirming the paper's "several percent" statement.
- **Two published PICA models disagree about the *sign* of the pressure effect on conductivity (I-V4).** The MEDLI2 material-response paper's Table 3 gives room-temperature conductivity at both 1 atm and 0.001 atm. The Heritage PICA model has virgin conductivity **rising** from 0.174 to 0.520 W/(m K) as pore-gas pressure falls — a threefold *increase*, the opposite of what a porous medium normally does. The MEDLI2 re-measurement has it **falling** from 0.169 to 0.127, the expected direction. At 0.001 atm the two differ by a factor of **4.1**, in exactly the regime that governs entry. A solver with pressure-independent conductivity — which ours was — can represent neither. Both rows are now implemented, interpolating in log-pressure between the published anchors and clamping rather than extrapolating beyond them; neither is presented as correct.
- **Real equilibrium B′ tables, and a solver ceiling that looks like data (I-V4).** FIAT's surface chemistry comes from ACE or MAT; neither is available, but Mutation++ is open source and computes the same multiphase equilibrium. Both it and the `Modified_Bprime` generator of Padovan et al. (2023) build here, and [`tools/generate-bprime-table.sh`](tools/generate-bprime-table.sh) produces a 15,505-state **TACOT-26** table — the open PICA surrogate — in about a second. The raw output contains a flat series of B′_c values (200, 210, … 500) that are the **solver's ceiling, not physics**: they track the B′_g grid rather than temperature and occupy 14% of the table. The right discriminator turned out not to be a magnitude threshold but the condensed-carbon mole fraction in the same row — exactly 1 where a graphite phase exists, 0 where the equilibrium has consumed it — and across 15,505 states that partition matches the capped rows *to the last entry*, so genuine B′_c values as high as 147 survive while every fictitious one is removed. The sublimation boundary is kept as a queryable surface rather than interpolated across, and behaves correctly: onset at 3100 K at 0.001 bar rising to 4000 K at 1 bar. Reading it also exposed a defect in our own smoothness diagnostic — `roughness()` normalised by the *local* B′_c, and a real table is essentially zero below the onset of surface chemistry, so it divided by those zeros and reported **1.6×10¹¹** for a surface that is smooth where it matters. Global normalisation gives 0.70.
- **FIAT's pyrolysis model form cannot reproduce PICA's measured response to heating rate (I-V4).** Two calibrated PICA models from Torres-Herrador et al. are now implemented: a six-reaction **parallel** set (2019, *J. Anal. Appl. Pyrolysis* 141:104625) that fits FIAT Eq. (8), and a **competitive** scheme (2020, *Aerosp. Sci. Technol.* 100:105784, with Bayesian uncertainties) that does not. Run at the two rates the latter was calibrated against — Wong et al. at 10 K/min, Bessire & Minton at 366 K/min — the parallel set moves its pyrolysis peak **+105 K** while the competitive scheme moves it **−129 K**. The measurement is downward: Stokes reported it above 300 K/min, and Torres-Herrador et al. state that parallel mechanisms are *"not able to reproduce this effect due to their mathematical formulation."* Eq. (8) is a sum of independent Arrhenius terms and each shifts up, so the sum can only shift up. **This is a model-form limitation, not a calibration error — no refitting removes it**, and it bites in flight rather than in the lab: heating rates across the MSL heat shield run 60 to 60,000 K/min while legacy TGA calibration rarely exceeds tens of K/min. Two cross-checks between unrelated sources pass: the competitive model's slow-branch char yield is 0.837 against 227/274 = 0.828 from the published bulk densities, and the 2019 set's density-loss fractions scaled by PICA's 94/274 resin fraction give 18.3% composite mass loss against the 17.2% those densities imply.
- **Groves Table 4.1 replaced my guessed IMU grades, and corrected the taxonomy as well as the numbers.** With the reference in hand the instrument grades are sourced rather than invented: Groves, *Principles of GNSS, Inertial, and Multisensor Integrated Navigation Systems* 2nd ed., Table 4.1, tabulates accelerometer and gyro bias by class. My guesses sat in roughly the right places under the wrong names — 'navigation' (50 ug, 0.01 deg/hr) lands inside his **aviation** band, and 'strategic' (5 ug) was twice as optimistic as his best listed grade, **marine**; there is no 'strategic' class in Groves at all. Derived injection error over a 300 s boost is now marine **11.6 m / 0.077 m/s**, aviation **44.8 m / 0.299 m/s**, intermediate **98.6 m / 0.677 m/s**, tactical **507 m / 3.74 m/s**, consumer **21.4 km / 214 m/s**. Adopting the real values also killed a coincidence: my invented marine and navigation grades happened to share a bias ratio, so a test asserted they share a gyro crossover. The real grades do not (10000 vs 3000), and that test now constructs the equal-ratio case explicitly rather than relying on an artefact of numbers I made up. Alignment is **not** in Table 4.1 — it depends on the alignment procedure rather than the instrument — so it remains a stated input and says so.
- **Boost injection is now derived from IMU grade, and it is *alignment* that dominates — not gyro drift.** The last stated specification in the chain now follows from three textbook propagation laws whose differing time exponents make the dominant term identifiable: accelerometer bias and initial misalignment integrate twice into position, gyro drift three times because the tilt it causes grows linearly first. Verified exactly — doubling the burn multiplies the first two by **4.000** and the third by **8.000**. For a 300 s boost: strategic grade **10.9 m / 0.073 m/s**, navigation **48.2 m / 0.322 m/s**, tactical **507 m / 3.74 m/s**. The intuitive answer is that gyro drift dominates; it does not, because over a few hundred seconds the cubic term has not had time to matter — gyro only overtakes accelerometer bias after **3094 s** at navigation grade. And the velocity term is the one that matters: position error at burnout is the smaller problem, because velocity error persists and the transfer that follows amplifies it by 850–3484 s. What remains an assumption is the IMU grade itself, which is a procurement decision rather than a physical constant, and it is labelled as such.
- **Bank reversals are free only with an infinite roll rate, and pricing them turns a reversal count into an actuator requirement.** The 0.43 km glide configuration needs 53 reversals, which I had flagged as an unmodelled cost. A reversal sweeps bank through **zero**, where the *vertical* lift component is maximum, so the vehicle lofts every time; a slower roll holds that excursion longer. Measured at the tightest deadband: **0.44/0.41 km at unlimited rate, 0.56/0.39 at 30 °/s, 0.62/0.92 at 15, 0.71/1.59 at 7.5, and 6.41/26.23 km at 3 °/s** — a collapse, not a degradation, and worse than not reversing at all. The cause shows in the reversal count *falling* as the rate drops: at 3 °/s a 120° reversal takes 40 s while the deadband demands one every 20, so the vehicle is permanently slewing and never holds a bank. **Requirement: ~15 °/s for sub-kilometre, ~30 °/s to preserve essentially all of the 0.43 km.** That signature — reversal count falling as authority drops — is now a test.
- **The terminal homing model is no longer a scaling — it is the linearised loop, verified against Zarchan.** The weakest link in the accuracy chain was a hand-rolled exponential decay standing in for homing-loop convergence. It is now the actual closed loop of Zarchan's *Tactical and Strategic Missile Guidance* Ch. 6 — `y'' = n_T − n_L`, `n_L' = (n_c − n_L)/τ`, `n_c = N'·ZEM/t_go²` — integrated directly, which for the linear model is exactly what his adjoint method computes. Verified against **three statements in the source**, not against itself: miss from an initial heading error vanishes for `t_F/τ ≳ 10` (0.005 m at 20); normalised miss from a step manoeuvre peaks at finite `t_F/τ` and decays rather than growing without bound; and **doubling the guidance time constant raises manoeuvre-induced miss by "more than an order of magnitude"** — measured **12.0×**. The exponential it replaced was smooth where the real response is not.
- **The crossrange deadband bounded the wrong invariant, and fixing it improved the glide dispersion 80× overall.** After the handover fix, crossrange dominated at 8.0 km — and the cause was diagnosable to within a few percent: the deadband is scheduled on speed across 7000→1000 m/s, a range-based handover ends the flight near **1713 m/s** with the deadband still at **3.19°**, and 150 km × tan(3.19°) = 8.4 km. A deadband on *heading error* is the wrong invariant; what matters is the lateral distance that error becomes. `crossrange_deadband_from_range` bounds the **miss** instead, `δ(R) = arctan(Δ/R)`. Note which way that runs — **tight far out, permissive close in**, the opposite of the speed schedule and of intuition, because a given heading error becomes a larger miss the further away it is held. (I wrote the prose backwards first; my own test assertion caught it.) The floor then binds, trading accuracy against reversal count: **3.7 km at 2° / 11 reversals, 1.3 km at 0.75° / 26, 0.43 km at 0.25° / 53.** Full progression for the glide: 34.0/35.1 km (drag tracking) → 11.3/6.7 (range loop) → 1.3/8.0 (range handover) → 0.40/1.30 (range-scheduled deadband) → 0.43/0.43 km at the tightest floor. **Eighty times better than drag tracking alone, and nearly circular.** Glide-architecture inertial CEP: 9020 → **1568 m**; required seeker acquisition for 100 m CEP: 150 km → **50 km**.
- **A speed gate ends the glide wherever the vehicle got to, not where the target is — fixing that cut downrange dispersion ninefold.** The residual after the range loop was downrange-dominated, and the cause was the *stopping condition*: terminating on speed is defined on the vehicle's own state, not on its relationship to the target, so even perfect range control cannot help once the vehicle runs out of energy in the wrong place. Handing over when range-to-go reaches the terminal acquisition range makes the arrival point *the handover point by construction*. Measured across the same 40-case Monte Carlo: drag tracking alone **34.0 / 35.1 km** → outer range loop **11.3 / 6.7 km** → range-based handover at 150 km **1.3 / 8.0 km**. Crossrange rises slightly in exchange because the bank-reversal logic is cut off mid-convergence — a real trade, not an artefact — leaving a strongly anisotropic 6:1 dispersion, which is exactly the regime where the circular CEP→R95 scaling misleads (ratio 2.780 here against the circular 2.079). Consequence: **required seeker acquisition drops from 150 km to 100 km** for a 100 m CEP, and the glide architecture's inertial CEP improves 9020 → 4907 m. A handover range the vehicle cannot reach falls back to the speed gate, which is how an under-ranging profile announces itself.
- **A midcourse correction is a *reset*, not a reduction — I had modelled it backwards.** Deriving the midcourse contribution produced a "reduction factor" greater than one, which exposed the error. A correction nulls the *trajectory* error outright; what survives is the error in the estimate it was computed from plus the error in executing it, and **neither depends on how large the incoming error was**. Modelling the phase as a multiplier on accumulated error makes a good correction look worse after a bad boost and better after a good one — exactly inverted. Measured with `correction_maneuver` carrying a navigation covariance and the bus execution model, the floor is **512 m at 200 m / 0.2 m/s navigation** and 1279 m at 500 m / 0.5 m/s. Pinned by a test asserting a twentyfold spread in boost error leaves the post-correction answer unchanged, and a complementary one confirming the reset is not applied to architectures that have no correction. Dispensing was derived the same way: a four-body sequence gives 30, 860, 1202, 784 m — the first body nearly free because it inherits only its own release error, the rest carrying everything the bus did before them.
- **Tracking a drag profile is not controlling range, and the parametric glide dispersion was wrong by a factor of twenty.** Deriving the dispersions rather than assuming them turned up a structural defect. Forty closed-loop glides with a 900 m entry-interface error and 3% ballistic-coefficient scatter gave **34 km downrange and 35 km crossrange** one-sigma — a *fortyfold amplification* of the entry error — against the 800 m / 250 m I had assumed. The cause: the drag tracker flies its reference profile faithfully and lands wherever that profile happens to reach, because nothing in the loop notices range error accumulating. Closing an outer loop on predicted range-to-go (the Shuttle-lineage fix, now `range_gain` on `simulate_glide`) cuts it to **11.3 km / 6.7 km** at gain 20 — 4× better overall, 5× in crossrange. The residual is downrange-dominated and does not shrink much further because the glide terminates on a *speed gate* rather than on arrival; handing over on a range-and-energy condition is the next structural fix and is **not** made. Consequence for accuracy: with the derived 11.3 km handover, reaching 100 m CEP needs seeker acquisition at **150 km**, not 50 km.
- **A shallow deorbit amplifies injection error four times more than a steep one.** Propagating a 200 m / 1 m/s injection covariance through the actual Kepler transfer via `miss_sensitivity` gives entry-interface principal sigmas of 915 / 639 / 630 m (1281 m RSS) — which happens to validate the 1200 m that had been assumed, but now derives it. The sensitivity itself is the finding: `|∂r_f/∂v_0|` runs **850 s at a −400 km virtual perigee against 3484 s at +50 km**, so the same perigee-depth choice that trades ΔV against transfer arc also trades error amplification, in the same direction. Deorbiting shallow is cheap in propellant and expensive in accuracy.
- **Reaching a small CEP is a survey problem before it is a guidance problem.** Terminal accuracy is now derived rather than parametric, and the model reports *which of four floors binds* — divert, seeker, loop convergence, or target location — because the remedies don't overlap. The instinct that seeker accuracy sets the answer is usually wrong: acquisition range enters through `t_go²` while angular accuracy enters only linearly and only at the freeze range, so **doubling acquisition range beats a tenfold better seeker**. Nulling a 1200 m handover error in the 3.3 s that 20 km buys at 6 km/s demands **33 g** of lateral acceleration, which a glide vehicle does not have. Measured path to the targets: 1 mrad seeker at **50 km** (not 20), **≥20 g** divert, and target location to **50 m** gives **CEP 58.9 m / R95 122 m**; tightening target location to 10 m gives **CEP 11.8 m / R95 24.5 m**; 3 m target location plus deceleration gives **CEP 3.5 m**. Past the third step every remaining gain is in survey and timekeeping, not guidance — and [geodesy.py](src/passes/geodesy.py) shows how easily that is lost (geodetic-vs-geocentric latitude is 21.4 km; a one-minute epoch error is 27.9 km). One result worth flagging: adding a seeker the vehicle **cannot exploit makes the answer worse** (1958 m vs 1263 m inertial-only), because the inertial fallback multiplier was flattering and the computed divert-limited answer is honest.
- **The standard CEP→R95 conversion factor under-states the 95% radius for every real dispersion.** Every architecture now reports terminal accuracy as both CEP and R95, computed from the exact elliptical containment integral rather than by scaling. The circular constant `R95/CEP = √(ln20/ln2) ≈ 2.0789` is only valid for an isotropic dispersion, which a terminal error ellipse is not — downrange and crossrange errors come from different mechanisms. Measured, the ratio **rises** with elongation: 2.079 at unit aspect ratio, 2.339 at 2:1, 2.646 at 3.3:1, approaching **2.906** in the one-dimensional limit, which is exactly the normal-distribution value 1.96/0.6745. Scaling a CEP by 2.079 therefore under-states R95 by 1.5–5% across the architectures tabulated and by up to 40% at the limit. (My own docstring first claimed the ratio *falls*, then contradicted itself in the next clause; the measurement settled it.) The radial part of the integral is analytic, leaving a 1-D quadrature, so the elliptical answers are exact rather than a fitted correction. **The per-phase error contributions are parametric inputs, not derived** — a CEP from this module is those numbers plus arithmetic, and only the arithmetic is verified.
- **An architecture can fail by overshooting, and that is a different diagnosis from falling short.** End-to-end budgets now charge every phase its range, ΔV and time and test the sum against the launch-site-to-aimpoint geometry. The structural point is that *which leg absorbs the remainder* differs between families: a fractional-orbital profile absorbs it in the **parking arc**, which costs time and no propellant, so closure only asks whether the remainder is non-negative and fits one revolution; a suborbital profile has no such leg, so it falls on **boost**, which is bought with propellant, and closure is tested against a stated booster. Treating those alike is the standard way to get a range budget wrong. For a 10,571 km required range: ballistic falls **8,271 km short** of a 2,000 km booster (infeasible for it), while FOBS+glide **overshoots** — fixed legs already cover 13,135 km, and no parking arc closes a negative remainder. The remedy is a shorter glide, not a bigger booster; 3,000 km of glide closes it exactly. The budget reports an unsigned magnitude plus a named reason rather than a signed residual, because the two failures are not opposite ends of one scale.
- **Mixed glide-and-ballistic deployments break the "one terminal regime" rule, and correctly so.** A bus can dispense glide vehicles and ballistic RVs on the same pass, at which point terminal regime stops being a property of the *architecture* and becomes a property of each *body* — the two classes fly different atmospheric arcs concurrently. My first rule set forbade this outright, which is right for a uniform payload and wrong in general. The corrected rule admits exactly {glide, ballistic} for a mixed payload, and reports a request for *the* terminal regime of a mixed architecture as the category error it is rather than silently returning the first. Because the arcs are concurrent their listed order carries no meaning, so glide is listed first by convention — without that, every mixed architecture enumerates twice (108 instead of 54). Total admissible sequences: **154**, of which 54 are mixed.
- **WGS-84 is the universal input format, and the epoch is not optional.** Launch sites and aimpoints are now specified as geodetic latitude/longitude/altitude and converted at the boundary. Three measured facts drove the design: geodetic and geocentric latitude differ by **21.4 km on the ground** at 45°; an equatorial ground point travels **465 m/s through the inertial frame**, so a one-minute epoch error displaces an aimpoint by 27.9 km — which is why `Aimpoint.from_geodetic` takes the *arrival* time and threads it through both the rotation and the targeting; and the polar radius is 21.4 km under the equatorial, so the spherical assumption in the glide and FOBS modules is a guidance-structure convenience that must be converted out at the boundary. Bowring's closed form alone degrades to **0.32 m at geostationary**, so it is seeded and refined twice: round-trip is then under 10 nm in-atmosphere and under a micrometre to geostationary.
- **The system taxonomy is derived, not tabulated — and deriving it removed 52 duplicate architectures.** Encoding the composition rules and enumerating from them separates "nobody builds it" from "not physically admissible", which a hard-coded table conflates. The rule that mattered most was one I initially got wrong: a glide vehicle *can* fly a purely ballistic entry by declining to use its lift, and a cruiser can glide with the engine off, so my first rule set admitted both — enumerating one physical trajectory twice under two payload labels, the second copy carrying a description its own flight contradicts. Requiring an architecture to *use* the capability that names it removes 72 such duplicates, taking the count from **226 to 154**. Breakdown: 12 single-RV, 36 multiple-RV, 12 glider, 36 multiple-glider, 54 mixed, 4 cruiser. All nine named reference architectures (ballistic ± multiple, boost-glide ± multiple, fractional-orbital × four payload classes, powered cruise) appear in the enumeration, and everything the enumerator produces independently re-validates — so it is a derivation rather than a curated list kept in parallel with its own rules.
- **"Fuel has diminishing returns, L/D does not" is backwards.** Bréguet range is logarithmic in *mass ratio*, and the usual gloss transfers that to fuel fraction. It doesn't. At Mach 8, doubling L/D from 4 to 8 multiplies range by exactly **2.00**; doubling fuel fraction from 0.30 to 0.60 multiplies it by **2.57**. `ln(1/(1-f))` is *convex* in f — its derivative `1/(1-f)` grows — so doubling f more than doubles the logarithm. The real diminishing return is in mass ratio: doubling `m_i/m_f` adds exactly `ln 2` whatever it was before. Different variables, and conflating them misprices tankage against aerodynamics. Also measured: the cruise-climb is exactly `H·ln(m_i/m_f)` = **3.03 km at 30% fuel, independent of the vehicle** — wing loading, L/D and C_L all cancel.
- **A reference drag profile has to be flyable, and constant drag is not (II-V11).** The equilibrium glide supports only about **2 m/s² at entry interface** — near orbital speed, centrifugal relief cancels most of the vehicle's weight, leaving little lift to spare for drag — rising to ~10 m/s² by handover. A constant 20 m/s² reference is therefore not a demanding command but an *impossible* one over the first half of the glide, and the tracker answers by sitting against its bank stop (100% saturation at 40 m/s², flown/commanded drag 0.40). Built from the equilibrium-glide condition instead, predicted and flown range agree to **6%** at moderate bank. Adding integral action first was treating the symptom: no gain creates authority that isn't there.
- **Bank magnitude and sign are independent in mechanism but not in use (II-V11).** Magnitude sets range, sign sets crossrange, and neither enters the other's law. But the lateral logic steers on *bearing to target*, so a longitudinal profile that overflies inverts that bearing mid-glide and the deadband degenerates. Range-matched, reversals cut terminal crossrange **39×** (1199 → 31 km); with the target 1000 km short of the delivered range, only **4×** — which is how V11 first failed. Range matching is a precondition for the lateral channel, not a separate concern.
- **Perigee depth, not the burn, is the dominant design choice in a fractional orbital profile.** The deorbit burn is cheap and buys timing rather than energy: from a 200 km circular orbit, dropping perigee to 50 km costs **45 m/s against 7784 m/s orbital speed — under 0.6%**. But how deep perigee goes governs everything downstream, and the trade is steep: across virtual perigees from +80 km to −1000 km, ΔV runs 35.9 → 401 m/s while the transfer arc falls 131° → 30° (14,629 → 3,395 km) and entry flight-path angle steepens −0.39° → −3.19°. An order of magnitude in ΔV buys about a factor of four in arc. The shallow end is not free either — a grazing perigee spends 14,600 km merely descending. The Kepler transfer solve agrees with the independent `propagate_coast` integrator to **machine precision** on radius, swept angle, speed and flight-path angle.
- **Deployment ordering dominates the maneuvers, and the natural order was the worst available.** A post-boost bus dispensing four independently targeted vehicles has an assignment problem sitting on top of continuous dynamics. Enumerating all 24 orderings for aimpoints spread ±100 km downrange and ±60 km crossrange gives an **85% spread** between cheapest and dearest — 405 against 748 m/s — and index order is not merely suboptimal, it is **the worst of the twenty-four**. Exhaustive search is affordable only for small fleets since each evaluation needs a full targeting solve per vehicle; above that the search is 2-opt and reports itself as a local optimum rather than an optimum.
- **The footprint anisotropy belongs to the timing freedom, not the geometry.** The standard claim is that downrange separation is cheap and crossrange expensive. Under a *fixed* arrival epoch it is false: displacing an aimpoint 50 km costs **35.1 m/s downrange against 40.4 m/s crossrange**, a 15% difference and not a qualitative one. The asymmetry appears only when arrival can absorb the displacement — letting it slip **10 s halves the downrange cost to 16.1 m/s** while leaving crossrange and radial costs *exactly* unchanged, their optima at zero slip. A plane change cannot be bought with time; a downrange displacement can. A mission specifying a common arrival epoch has therefore priced downrange separation as though it were crossrange, probably without intending to.
- **Execution error does not penalise correcting early, and terminal dispersion does not follow release order.** Two intuitions we held going in, both wrong. Execution error scales with the maneuver, the maneuver scales as $1/t_{go}$, and terminal sensitivity scales as $t_{go}$ — **the two cancel exactly**: with navigation error zeroed, the execution-induced miss runs 97.6 → 91.6 m across a nineteen-fold range of time-to-go over which the maneuver grows seventeen-fold. What favours a late burn is knowledge alone, and expected miss falls monotonically to the end of the arc even at 100 mrad pointing error, so burn timing is a genuinely two-objective trade rather than a scalar optimisation with an interior optimum. Separately, a bus's accumulated covariance grows monotonically — each maneuver adds a positive-semidefinite block and none is removed — but the **dispersions do not**, running 1482, 1983, 1744 m over three vehicles, because a later release has less flight time for its inherited error to become a miss.
- **The pyrolysis-gas composition we derived from measurement violates conservation of mass, and FIAT's own published value does not (I-V4).** Two independent routes — Torres-Herrador's fitted species yields, and Bessire & Minton's integrated mass spectrometry — agreed on C:H:O = 0.1745:0.6785:0.1470, and that agreement was treated as confirmation. It is not: both are gas-phase speciation measurements sharing the same bias. Scoggins (2017) Eq. (12) writes phenolic pyrolysis as one step producing a pure-carbon char, so hydrogen and oxygen pass through untouched and the gas **must** inherit the resin's H/O ratio of 6.04. Every published composition sits within 2.3% of it; ours sits **23.6% below**. Inverting the balance, ours demands a char with H/O ≈ 13 — hydrogen-rich — and at any char yield above 50% it demands *negative* oxygen. Both measured chars (Sykes 2.2, Tran ≈ 0) are oxygen-rich relative to the resin, so removing char can only drive the gas H/O *up*. No char can produce our number. Rabinovitch (AIAA 2014-2246) §III.A.2 reports FIATv3's own value, C:H:N:O:Si = 0.18:0.68:0.014:0.12:0.006, from a paper that states explicitly that no export-controlled data was used; that is now what the PICA B′ table is built on. Scoggins' framing also grades the candidates by how far the char yield inferred through hydrogen differs from the one inferred through oxygen: **Park 0.1 points, TACOT 0.5, FIATv3 1.1, ours 4.1**. As a by-product his stated maximum char yields (79.2% linear, 80.4% cross-linked) reproduce from our stored resin composition as 79.3% and 80.3%, confirming it from a source that did not supply it.
- **Which arc-jet enthalpy to pair with the calibration heat flux is decidable, and it was worth four points (I-V4).** Milos & Chen report two enthalpies per condition, a facility correlation and a DPLR value, differing by up to 45%; the choice had been left open. Zoby's empirical stagnation-point correlation settles it. Inverting $q_s\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}/p_s} = K_{\mathrm{mix}}(H_s - H_w)$ against the tabulated $q$ and $p$ recovers an effective radius of **9.0 cm on 15 of the 19 conditions** — across a decade in heat flux, 36× in pressure, five nozzle exits and two facilities. That consistency identifies the correlation the facility used to *produce* its enthalpy column, so $q_{cw}$ and the facility enthalpy are two outputs of one calculation on one calibration measurement and belong together. DPLR's value comes from an independent CFD solution; composing it with a calibration-derived heat flux biases every case the same way. Switching improves median error from 8% to 4% (TACOT) and 10% to 7% (PICA), and is a physics argument rather than a fit.
- **The residual is one-signed and monotone in oxygen, which points somewhere specific (I-V4).** Both equilibrium tables over-predict recession on nearly every case. Sweeping the pyrolysis-gas oxygen fraction across the three defensible compositions — 0.147, 0.122, 0.115 — moves the mean signed error almost linearly from **+9.0% to +4.8% to +1.1%**. A bias surviving a decade in heat flux and 36× in pressure is not table noise, and it extrapolates to zero near an oxygen fraction of 0.113, essentially TACOT's. This is *not* evidence that the surrogate describes PICA better: it is one inverse inference through a solver carrying its own errors in kinetics and conductivity slopes, any of which could absorb the same bias. What it does establish is that the surrogate's remaining advantage sits in a single identified input rather than anywhere diffuse.
- **A NASA-9 parsing bug, not the solver, was why in-house equilibrium never converged (I-V4).** The native Gibbs minimiser failed at *every* state, which had been recorded as a solver defect. It was not. NASA-9 reserves the eighth coefficient slot and leaves it unused; Gurvich-era records spell that as blanks, while species added later write an explicit `0.000000000E+00`. Compacting non-blank fields rather than reading positionally shifts the two integration constants one slot left on exactly the latter group — substituting 0 for the enthalpy constant and the enthalpy constant for the entropy constant. C5 is such a record, and the corrupted parse gave it $s/R = 147{,}965$ against a true 54.4, so $\exp(-g/RT)$ overflowed and poisoned the Hessian before the first step. A second, genuine defect sat behind it: Newton on a sum of exponentials descends by exactly **one e-fold per iteration** from far away, so the old formulation needed ~120 iterations merely to reach the right order of magnitude and hit its cap. Recast with log-space residuals and solved jointly in the element potentials and the total moles, it converges in **4–8 iterations** and holds the elemental composition to 1 part in $10^{8}$; a temperature-continuation fallback covers 95 of 98 states from 300–6000 K and $10$–$10^{7}$ Pa. The three that remain are at 300 K, where a gas-only model genuinely cannot represent a mixture whose carbon should have condensed, and it now says so instead of returning a plausible-looking answer. Species built from an element absent from the requested composition are also dropped rather than left unconstrained — nothing in $An = b$ holds N₂ down when no nitrogen is supplied.

## Technical summary

What follows condenses the papers. Where the two disagree with each other, the papers say so explicitly and explain why.

### Structural discretization

Paper I discretizes the variable-rigidity Euler–Bernoulli beam by dense Chebyshev collocation, retaining the full product-rule expansion:

$$\mathbf{K} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{EI})\,\mathbf{D}^4 + 2\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{EI})\,\mathbf{D}^3 + \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{D}^2\mathbf{EI})\,\mathbf{D}^2$$

Dropping the second and third terms is only valid where $EI$ is near-constant over a bending wavelength, which fails across stage joints and in regions of thermal softening.

Paper II replaces this. Dense collocation conditions as $\mathcal{O}(N^{2k})$ — that is $\mathcal{O}(N^8)$ for fourth-order bending — which is manageable in 1D but not for a bivariate tensor-product operator. Paper II uses the **ultraspherical spectral method**, where differentiation is banded and conditioning is $\mathcal{O}(1)$, with the bivariate system assembled as a Kronecker-structured generalized Sylvester equation.

Paper II also replaces Euler–Bernoulli kinematics with **Mindlin–Reissner** three-field plates $(w, \phi_x, \phi_y)$. Transverse shear is not a correction for thick hull sections — it is the dominant mechanism of the torsional modes that set flutter margins. This requires three independent free-edge conditions ($M_x = M_{xy} = Q_x = 0$) rather than the classical Kirchhoff effective-shear pair, which over-constrains the perimeter.

### The stiffness constraint

This is stated rather than assumed away, because it is the sharpest limit on the architecture. For the spectral structural operator, $\lambda_{\max}$ inherits the $\mathcal{O}(N^8)$ growth of the fourth-derivative operator, so $\omega_{\max} = \mathcal{O}(N^4)$, and any explicit Runge–Kutta method requires

$$\Delta t \le C_{\mathrm{RK}} / \omega_{\max} = \mathcal{O}(N^{-4})$$

At $N = 32$ that factor is $\sim 10^6$. Continuity does not rescue you from this. Paper I gives two mitigations — modal truncation and IMEX splitting, the latter preserving the batching argument because the factorization is shared across all replicates — and V3 measures which is preferable.

### Aerothermodynamics

Stagnation convective heating follows **Fay–Riddell**, with the Lewis exponent stated ($\beta = 0.52$ equilibrium, $0.63$ frozen/catalytic) and the stagnation velocity gradient supplied by the modified Newtonian estimate. **Tauber–Sutton** gives the radiative component, and **Lees** the distribution away from stagnation.

Note the trade the papers make explicit: recession increases $R_\mathrm{eff}$, which *reduces* convective heating as $R_\mathrm{eff}^{-1/2}$ but *increases* radiative heating. A framework modeling only convection will systematically favor over-blunted geometries.

Ablation is split by material class rather than forced into one model — charring pyrolysis for phenolic acreage, single-temperature oxidative recession for non-pyrolyzing refractory leading edges (C/C, ZrB₂–SiC). Applying either model to the other material class is wrong in a specific, stated way.

### Navigation and guidance

Anomaly detection gates on the normalized innovation squared, $d_k^2 = \bm{\nu}_k^\top \mathbf{S}_k^{-1} \bm{\nu}_k \sim \chi^2_m$, against a design false-alarm rate. On detection, IAE inflates process noise by a **bounded** scalar trace ratio $\alpha_k \in [1, \alpha_{\max}]$.

Paper I is explicit that positive-semidefiniteness of $\mathbf{Q}^*_k$ follows trivially from scaling a PSD matrix by a non-negative scalar — *not* from the trace clamp, which says nothing about matrix definiteness. What makes the scheme unconditionally well-posed is that it estimates a **scalar** rather than a matrix; entrywise estimators of $\mathbf{Q}$ routinely return indefinite matrices under short windows.

Terminal guidance uses the numerically stable time-to-go root:

$$t_{go} = \frac{2 R_\mathrm{LOS}}{\hat V_c + \sqrt{\hat V_c^2 + 2 \hat A_c R_\mathrm{LOS}}}$$

This is algebraically identical to the textbook quadratic form but does not lose precision as $\hat A_c \to 0$ — which is where terminal guidance spends most of its time. The textbook form evaluates $0/0$ by catastrophic cancellation there. A guard handles the negative-discriminant case (decelerating closure predicts no intercept), which is physically meaningful and must not propagate as NaN.

Paper II's SCvx layer uses **free** virtual controls under an exact $\ell_1$ penalty. Sign-constraining them makes the subproblem infeasible in exactly the cases they exist to rescue. The $\ell_1$ norm is chosen because it is *exact* — above a finite penalty weight the virtual controls reach zero exactly, where a quadratic penalty only drives them to zero as $w_\nu \to \infty$.

For plasma blackout, the unaided covariance grows as

$$\mathbf{P}_{rr}(t) \sim \tfrac{1}{3} q_a t^3 + \tfrac{1}{4}\sigma_{b_a}^2 t^4 + \tfrac{1}{36} g^2 \sigma_{b_g}^2 t^6$$

in the velocity-random-walk, accelerometer-bias, and gyro-bias-through-gravity channels. The $t^6$ term dominates past a few tens of seconds. Quadratic is the growth of position *error* from an accelerometer bias, not of the *covariance* — a guidance layer sizing its pull-up trigger on a quadratic model will under-predict badly at the durations that matter.

### Dispersion statistics

Terminal footprints are characterized by the eigendecomposition of the sample impact covariance. $R_{95}$ uses $\chi^2_{2,0.95} = 5.991$, giving semi-axes $2.4477\,\sigma_i$. CEP uses the linear approximation $0.5887(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)$ — **but only where $0.25 \le \sigma_2/\sigma_1 \le 1$**, which lifting reentry footprints routinely violate. Outside that range the framework falls back to the direct order statistic rather than reporting a CEP the approximation does not support.

Relative standard error on each $\sigma_i$ is $\approx 1/\sqrt{2N_\mathrm{MC}}$ — 0.7% at $N_\mathrm{MC} = 10^4$. Any dispersion figure quoted without a sample size, or to more significant figures than that bound supports, is not meaningful.

### Blocked on external material (not code)

14. **FIAT ablation reference (I-V4).** ~~Needs the FIAT code~~ — **largely addressed.** `passes.thermal.fiat` implements FIAT's published formulation independently (Chen & Milos 1999 Eqs. 1–11; Milos, Chen & Squire 2006), with multilayer stacks, both in-depth radiation treatments, pressure-dependent conductivity from MEDLI2 Table 3, depth probes, environment scaling, ply-thickness optimization, published PICA kinetics (Torres-Herrador 2019/2020), and **real equilibrium B′ tables** generated from Mutation++ + TACOT-26. [`docs/FIAT-fidelity.md`](docs/FIAT-fidelity.md) audits it feature-by-feature. Two things remain: the **oxyacetylene-torch PICA validation case** of Quinn et al. (thermocouple depths and calibrated boundary conditions are tabulated — the first genuine validation data this project has had), and a native B′ solver (item 24).
15. **Fay–Riddell reference conditions (II-V8, convective half).** Needs transcribed equilibrium-air properties ($\rho_e\mu_e$, $\rho_w\mu_w$, $h_D$) at the published reference states. The radiative half is closed against the Tauber–Sutton source and the convective *coefficients* are now checked against the Fay & Riddell original, so what is left is a tabulated reference *case*. The original presents its results in figures (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 7, 8), not tables, and cannot supply one at the precision a 5% criterion needs; a later source with tabulated reference conditions is required.
16. **Achieved occupancy and warp divergence (I-V8).** Nsight Compute is installed and the code path is wired, but the driver returns `ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM` for a non-root user. Enabling counters means loading the NVIDIA module with `NVreg_RestrictProfilingToAdminUsers=0` — a host-wide security setting, deliberately not changed from here.

### Uncovered by implementation, and currently limiting

17. **Boundary-row preconditioning.** II-V1 measured the banded ultraspherical interior at $\mathcal{O}(1)$ and the *bordered* system at $N^{1.4}$–$N^{3.3}$. The conditioning claim the method is sold on therefore belongs to the interior, not to what actually gets solved. Any statement about scaling this discretization to large $N$ is a statement about the boundary treatment, and nothing here preconditions it.
18. **Corner-resolving refinement for free edges.** Free-edge plates carry weak corner singularities, so the free-free spectrum converges *algebraically* where the simply-supported spectrum converges exponentially. Until this is addressed, II-V3's free-free leg cannot carry a pass criterion at attainable $N$, and the verdict rests on the closed-form simply-supported reference. Graded meshes or singular enrichment would close it.
19. **Variable-coefficient bivariate operators.** Paper II specifies $\mathbf{D}_{ij}(\xi,\eta)$ built by hyperbolic blending; the plate kernel currently uses constant coefficients. Separable fields drop straight into the Kronecker assembly; genuinely bivariate ones need a low-rank approximation of each coefficient for the Sylvester structure to survive.
20. **Enthalpy-form thermal state.** Paper I, Eq. (3.20) carries nodal *enthalpy* precisely because it stays continuous across the pyrolysis zone where $c_p$ varies sharply. The charring solver integrates temperature, which is what the coupled state currently carries. Converting is a caloric-equation-of-state inversion, and the argument for doing it is strongest exactly where the current form is weakest.
21. **Batched coupled trajectories.** The batch layer and the coupled simulator both exist but have not been composed: the fixed-dimension state makes a rank-3 ensemble of *full* trajectories available, which is the framework's headline claim at full strength rather than on the reduced entry model I-V7/V8 use.

### Numerical practice worth promoting to first-class support

22. **Constraint stabilization as a framework service.** Baumgarte stabilization is used for the quaternion norm (Paper I) and, as the coupled simulator forced, for the thermal boundary conditions. Both are hand-rolled. A single tested facility for "differentiate the constraint, stabilize the drift" would generalize to the plate edge conditions and the free-free projection.
23. **Per-block tolerance and scaling policy.** A scalar `atol` costs three orders of magnitude on a state spanning nine orders of magnitude in units, and a block condition number is meaningless without its scaling stated. Both are currently conventions inside two modules rather than a policy the framework enforces.

24. **Native equilibrium B′ solver.** Mutation++ closes the B′ gap but fixes the material through a deck we do not control, so a **PICA** table — as opposed to a TACOT one — needs our own multiphase equilibrium. [`src/passes/thermal/fiat/equilibrium.py`](src/passes/thermal/fiat/equilibrium.py) has the formulation: NASA-9 thermodynamics (1262 species parsed, all 26 TACOT species present), the elemental surface balance, and carbon saturation as the closure. **The Gibbs solve does not yet converge** — the element-potential dual is the right formulation, but the cold-start guess is hundreds of decades off and the damping then crawls. Verification target is already in hand: the Mutation++ TACOT table, species-by-species.
25. **PICA elemental pyrolysis composition.** The only thing separating a TACOT B′ table from a PICA one is three lines of elemental composition. Torres-Herrador 2019 Table 2's ζ column gives per-reaction species yields (H₂O, CO, CO₂, C₆H₅OH, C₇H₈O, CH₄, C₆H₆, H₂, …), from which the elemental mass fractions follow directly. Blocked only on item 24.
26. **Oxyacetylene-torch validation (I-V4, closing).** Quinn et al. tabulate thermocouple depths (Table 1) and calibrated boundary conditions ρ_e u_e C_H and h_r at 250/500/750 W/cm² (Table 2), with temperature histories and recession measured. This satisfies [`docs/FIAT-reference-data.md`](docs/FIAT-reference-data.md) §1.2 and is the first real validation case available — **not** FIAT, but measurements, which is better.

## Scope and compliance

This repository contains mathematical formulation only: published governing equations, engineering-level correlations drawn from the open literature (Fay–Riddell, Lees, Tauber–Sutton, Sutton–Graves, CMA), and standard estimation and optimal control methods. Every source is cited and independently verifiable — see [`CITATION-AUDIT.md`](CITATION-AUDIT.md).

It contains **no** proprietary, classified, or export-controlled data: no vehicle geometries, no material property databases, no performance specifications, no validated aerodynamic tables, and no parameters traceable to any specific fielded system. The intended use is study of the numerical coupling between continuum mechanics and statistical estimation theory.

Under ITAR, information already published and generally accessible to the public is not "technical data" (22 CFR §120.34, public domain). Note that the separate *fundamental research* provision is defined with reference to accredited U.S. institutions of higher learning and does not straightforwardly cover independent researchers — the two are frequently conflated and are not the same exemption. **This is a description of intent, not legal advice.** If you are extending this work with real vehicle data or fielded-system parameters, get an actual export-control determination first.
